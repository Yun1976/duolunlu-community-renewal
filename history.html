History · 历史

A street with three lives街史三幕

Duolun Road has been a colonial road, a literary crucible, and a restored cultural street. Few places anywhere compress so much history into 550 metres.

1911
A road beyond the walls of the Settlement

The Shanghai Municipal Council — the municipal authority of the Shanghai International Settlement — lays out a new road just beyond the Settlement's northern boundary. Like other "extra-settlement roads" (越界筑路), it is built outside the formal boundary but under the Settlement's extraterritorial administration. It is named Darroch Road (窦乐安路), after John Darroch, a British missionary to China who had been received by the Guangxu Emperor. The street is laid out in an L, 550 metres end to end, both ends meeting North Sichuan Road.

1920s
Merchants build, writers move in

Darroch Road grows into a quiet residential street of lane houses and substantial villas. Chinese industrialists build large private residences — later bought up by political and military figures of the era — while the surrounding blocks fill with dense lilong housing, including classic shikumen (stone-gate) lanes like Jingyun Li. In 1928, the striking Chinese-Western fusion church Hongde Tang (Fitch Memorial Church) is completed.

1930
The League of Left-Wing Writers is founded on this street

On 2 March 1930, at the former campus of China Art University (中华艺术大学) on the street, the League of Left-Wing Writers (中国左翼作家联盟) is founded — the organisation that anchored modern Chinese left-wing literature. Lu Xun, who lives in Jingyun Li at No. 135 Lane, is its guiding spirit. Around the street and its lanes cluster Guo Moruo, Mao Dun, Ye Shengtao, Ding Ling, Rou Shi and dozens of other writers, editors and thinkers; the nearby Gongfei Café and the Uchiyama Bookstore on North Sichuan Road serve as their informal salons. This is the street's golden age — a centre of thought and literature whose influence ran far beyond Shanghai.

1943
A new name: Duolun

Wartime administration renames the street Duolun Road (多伦路), after Duolun county in Inner Mongolia. The great names move on or pass away, and the street settles back into being an ordinary residential lane of Hongkou for the next half century.

1998–1999
Rediscovery: the Street of Cultural Celebrities

A renewed interest in Republican-era history leads the local government to launch a regeneration project in 1998: historic buildings are conserved and restored, streetscape and sculptures added, and buildings converted to museums, galleries, cafés and craft shops. The street — by now promoted as the Duolun Road Cultural Celebrities Street (多伦路文化名人街) — is closed to traffic; pedestrianisation is completed in 1999. The Duolun Museum of Modern Art opens as part of the project.

Today
A working cultural street

Duolun Road today is a pedestrian street of galleries, antique and ceramics shops, a bookshop, a guqin studio, cafés and community exhibition spaces — anchored by memorial sites to its literary past and sustained by the neighbourhood around it. Seasonal cultural programmes, an oral-history project recording the people of the street, and a slow rhythm of browsing and tea keep the century-long story going.

The people of the street

Beyond the League founders, the street and its immediate blocks housed a remarkable cast across the 1920s–40s: writers Lu Xun, Guo Moruo, Mao Dun, Ye Shengtao, Wang Zaoshi, Ding Ling, Rou Shi and Qu Qiubai; the Islamic-style mansion of the financier H.H. Kung (No. 250); No. 210, home for a time to general Bai Chongxi — where his son, the novelist Pai Hsien-yung, spent part of his childhood; and the French neoclassical residence associated with Tang Enbo. The memorial plaque system along the street records who lived where.
